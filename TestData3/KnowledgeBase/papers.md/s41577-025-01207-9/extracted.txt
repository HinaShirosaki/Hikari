Fifty years of monoclonals:
the past, present and future
of antibody therapeutics
Andrew C. Chan 1 , Greg D. Martyn 2 & Paul J. Carter 2
Abstract
In 1975, Köhler and Milstein invented hybridoma technology for the
generation of murine monoclonal antibodies with predetermined
antigen-binding specificity. The transformative impact of monoclonal
antibodies is demonstrated by their ubiquitous use as biomedical
research reagents and the worldwide approval of at least 212 antibody
therapeutics with tens of millions of patients treated to date. Advances
in antibody technologies, such as humanization and robust methods
for human antibody generation, mitigated the major limitations of
murine antibodies as therapeutics. These technologies, combined
with progress in biomanufacturing, helped to launch this modern era
of antibody therapeutics. Beyond IgG, antibody therapeutics have
blossomed into multiple alternative formats, including bispecific
antibodies and antibody–drug conjugates. Additionally, antibody
fragments have been developed as stand-alone therapeutics and
to target cell therapies, notably chimeric antigen receptor T cells.
These advances in antibody technologies, plus innovation enabling
subcutaneous delivery, have improved the therapeutic benefits
and convenience of antibody treatment for many patients. This
concept is illustrated here by multiple generations of antibody
therapeutics for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)+
cancers and B cell-targeted therapies for haematological cancers and
immunological diseases. Finally, we opine briefly on some of the many
promising future directions with antibody therapeutics, including the
application of artificial intelligence for antibody identification and
multi-parameter optimization.

Key steps enabling the rise of antibodies
as therapeutics
The clinical development time required for innovative therapeutics
varies greatly, with a median of approximately 8 years14. Eleven years
elapsed after the invention of hybridoma technology until the approval
of the first antibody, muromonab-CD3, by the FDA. Muromonab-CD3
targets CD3 and was approved in 1986 for treatment of acute kidney
transplant rejection (Fig. 2a). However, antibodies only started to take
off as a major new therapeutic modality in the late 1990s (Fig. 2a). This
delay reflects the multiple diverse problems that had to be solved
for antibodies to be developed as therapeutics (Fig. 1b). First, basic
research was required to investigate the pathobiology of human dis-
eases to identify relevant targets for therapeutic intervention with
monoclonal antibodies. Preclinical development followed to assess the
safety and efficacy of these antibodies. Early clinical testing of murine
antibodies demonstrated very limited efficacy and revealed substan-
tial shortcomings for human therapy: immunogenicity, short plasma
half-life and inefficient effector functions (Box 1). Reverse translation
of these insights from the clinic led to the advent of technologies that
had the potential to mitigate the limitations of murine antibodies.
In due course, murine antibodies redesigned through chimerization4,5
or humanization6–8 or, later, human antibodies (Box 1), were evaluated
for their pharmacokinetics, safety and efficacy in clinical trials. The first
chimeric antibody, abciximab, targets glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (also known
as integrin αIIbβIII), and was approved in 1994 to prevent cardiac ischae-
mic complications in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary
intervention15 (Fig. 2c). The first humanized antibody, daclizumab,
targets CD25 and was approved in 1997 in combination with cyclosporin
and corticosteroids for prevention of organ rejection in renal transplant
recipients16 (Fig. 2c). The first human IgG antibody, adalimumab, tar-
gets tumour necrosis factor (TNF) and was initially approved in 2002
for rheumatoid arthritis17 (Fig. 2c). Adalimumab has been one of the

Introduction
In 1975, Georges Köhler and César Milstein1 published their ground-
breaking invention of hybridoma technology for the generation of
monoclonal antibodies in Nature. Mice were immunized with a target
antigen of interest emulsified with an adjuvant to stimulate an immune
response (Fig. 1a). B cells isolated from the immunized mice were then
fused with myeloma cells to create immortalized cells known as ‘hybri-
domas’. These hybridoma cells were then screened to identify those
that secreted antibodies that selectively bound to the target antigen1.
A defining feature of hybridoma technology is that it provides a perpet-
ual source of antibodies that bind to a target antigen commonly, albeit
not invariably2, with exquisite selectivity and minimal cross-reactivity to
unrelated antigens. Within a few years monoclonal antibodies derived
from immunized mice and rats became widely adopted as specific bind-
ing reagents of choice for biomedical research and nowadays are ubiq-
uitously used for this purpose. Beyond research reagents, hybridoma
was a foundational technology that ultimately led to the current era
of antibody therapeutics. The vast potential of hybridoma technology
was quickly realized and led to the 1984 Nobel Prize for Physiology or
Medicine being awarded to Georges Köhler and César Milstein “for the
discovery of the principle for production of monoclonal antibodies”3.
This Nobel Prize was shared with Niels Jerne “for theories concerning
the specificity in development and control of the immune system”3.
More broadly, at least six Nobel Prizes and six Lasker Awards have been
made for ground-breaking contributions related to antibodies and
antibody therapeutics (Supplementary Table 1).
The transformative impact of hybridoma technology over the past
50 years is hard to overstate. Indeed, hybridoma provided a founda-
tion that has led to at least 212 approved antibody therapeutics to date
that have benefited millions of patients (see Antibody Society website,
a meticulously curated and up-to-date tabulation of approved and
registrational stage antibody therapeutics). Additionally, the term
‘monoclonal antibody’ or ‘monoclonal antibodies’ is cited in >348,000
scientific publications (PubMed) and in the claims of >15,800 issued US
patents. In this Review we celebrate the 50th anniversary of hybridoma
technology1 by discussing the past, present and future of antibody
therapeutics.
From a past perspective, key advances beyond hybridoma that
contributed to the rise of antibodies as therapeutics are highlighted
(Fig. 1b). These include antibody engineering methods, such as
chimerization4,5 and humanization6–8, and robust technologies for
human antibody discovery that largely overcame the substantial short-
comings of murine antibodies as therapeutics (Box 1). Additionally,
antibody engineering has been widely used to improve existing prop-
erties of antibodies, including modulation of Fc-mediated functions9,
as well as endowing antibodies with brand new properties, such as
bispecificity9,10. Advances in biomanufacturing were also crucial to
the clinical development and commercialization of antibodies (Box 2).
The landscape of antibody therapeutics has evolved from primarily
IgG to numerous additional formats, including bispecific antibodies,
antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) and chimeric antigen receptor
(CAR) T cells (Fig. 2a).
Transitioning from the past to present antibody therapeutics,
we illustrate the power of reverse translation. Specifically, insights
from the clinic in combination with advances in antibody technolo-
gies have been used to guide the design of antibody therapeutics that
provide greater clinical efficacy, improved patient safety and/or greater
convenience to patients. This is exemplified here by reviewing multiple
generations of antibody therapeutics targeting B cells — often with

Evolution of the antibody therapeutics landscape
The landscape of approved antibody therapeutics has grown to at least
212 as of May 2025 (see Antibody Society data) (Fig. 2a) and is expand-
ing rapidly with 18 or more new approvals per year since 2022 (ref. 20).
Most antibody therapeutics are approved for indications in oncology
(48%), or in autoimmunity and inflammatory diseases (25%) (Fig. 2b).
However, a growing number of antibodies have been approved for use
in other therapeutic areas, including infectious diseases, haematology,
neurology, metabolic diseases, ophthalmology and musculoskeletal
diseases (Antibody Society data and ref. 10) (Fig. 2b). Very few approved
antibody therapeutics are murine (4%, excluding use in CAR T cells)
(Fig. 2c). Indeed, most antibody therapeutics are humanized (51%)
or chimeric (8%) versions of murine antibodies. However, a growing
number of antibody therapeutics are human (34%) mainly from trans-
genic mice, but additionally from other sources, including phage dis-
play libraries (for example, adalimumab, belimumab, atezolizumab,
avelumab and necitumumab), yeast display libraries (for example,
tafolecimab) and human B cells (for example, the following anti-severe
acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) antibodies:
amubarvimab, romlusevimab, tixagevimab, cilgavimab, bamlanivimab
and etesevimab).
Almost all current antibody therapeutics are approved for deliv-
ery either by i.v. infusion (~63%), s.c. injection (~25%) or both i.v. and
s.c. (8%) (Fig. 2d). Intravitreal injection is used for a few antibody
therapeutics for the treatment of age-related macular degeneration,
for example, ranibizumab and brolucizumab-dbll, which target vas-
cular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and the bispecific antibody
faricimab-svoa (targets VEGF and angiopoietin 2). Additional, but
rarely used antibody delivery routes include intramuscular injection,
for example, palivizumab for respiratory syncytial virus infections and
tixagevimab plus cilgavimab for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)
and intraperitoneal infusion for catumaxomab for malignant ascites.
Antibody therapeutics have been dominated from the outset by
monospecific IgG, which represent ~74% of currently approved anti-
bodies (see Antibody Society data) (Fig. 2a). However, the past decade
has witnessed rapid growth of alternative antibody formats (Fig. 2a),
particularly ADCs21 (Fig. 2e) and bispecific antibodies22,23 (Fig. 2f).

Antibody–drug conjugates
The concept of arming antibodies through conjugation to protein tox-
ins, known as immunotoxins, originated in the early 1970s28. Pioneering
work involved chemical conjugation of antibodies to potent protein
payloads such as diphtheria toxin to induce cytotoxicity against malig-
nant cells28. This research laid the groundwork for subsequent devel-
opments in ADCs a few years later21. The core underlying concept of
ADCs is to use the targeting specificity of antibodies to enhance the
therapeutic index of highly cytotoxic small molecules by increasing
on-tumour activity and reducing unwanted off-target effects. The real-
world experience with ADCs in patients is much more complex21,29.
Indeed, clinical trials with many different radiolabelled antibodies
revealed that only a tiny percentage of administered antibodies accrues
in the tumour (≤0.1% injected dose per gram of tumour) with most
being catabolized elsewhere in the body30; presumably also the case
with ADCs31. Nevertheless, ADCs radically extend the plasma half-life
of small-molecule drugs, provide some enhancement of their thera-
peutic index and allow some preferential localization to tumour cells.
ADC binding to cell surface antigens induces their internalization,
typically through the endolysosomal pathway, and subsequently
releases their cytotoxic payloads in lysosomes. The payloads then
need to exit lysosomes by passive diffusion or active transport to reach
the sites of action, such as microtubules in the cytosol, or to cross the
nuclear membrane to act on DNA in the nucleus. These cellular uptake
mechanisms allow ADCs to deliver cell-impermeable payloads, such
as monomethyl auristatin F32, in addition to cell-permeable payloads,
such as the closely related monomethyl auristatin E33. The mechanisms
of payload release from the ADC depend upon the design of the linker
(cleavable or non-cleavable) and include proteolysis of the antibody
and/or linker, reduction of disulfide bonds or hydrolysis of acid-labile
bonds as discussed in detail elsewhere21. For non-cleavable linkers, the
mechanism of drug release is proteolytic catabolism of the entire ADC
to release active catabolites.
In 2000, the first ADC was approved by the FDA, namely, gemtu-
zumab ozogamicin, a humanized anti-CD33 antibody covalently linked
to the highly cytotoxic antibiotic, N-acetyl calicheamicin, through
an acid-labile linker. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin binds to CD33, an
antigen expressed in ~90% of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) cells.
Gemtuzumab ozogamicin internalization leads to release of cali-
cheamicin, which induces double-stranded DNA breaks that induce
AML cell death. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin was approved for the treat-
ment of relapsed AML34. However, it was withdrawn in 2010 when con-
firmatory trials failed to demonstrate clinical benefit and revealed
serious drug-associated adverse events including sometimes fatal
hepatotoxicity and veno-occlusive disease. Gemtuzumab ozogamicin
was re-approved by the FDA in 2017 with a lower fractionated dosing
schedule that improved the safety profile while providing clinical
efficacy35.
The approximately 30-year lag between the advent of immuno-
toxins28 and the approval of the first ADC reflects decades of inno-
vation across a broad range of disciplines to make ADCs beneficial
to patients. ADCs are inherently complex therapeutics, including
their pharmacology and manufacture, as they combine the intrica-
cies of protein (antibody) and small-molecule (payload and linker)
drug development21. A plethora of crucial decisions are required in
the design of successful ADCs, including the choice of target antigen,
antibody, cytotoxic payload, linker and drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR),
as well as the conjugation chemistry and sites of drug attachment, as

Box 1 | Mitigating the clinical limitations
of murine monoclonal antibodies
The high initial expectations of the therapeutic potential of murine
antibodies were quickly dashed in the 1980s by a paucity of clinical
benefit239,240 and the identification of three major and common
shortcomings. First, murine antibodies are highly immunogenic
foreign proteins, and in most patients they elicit anti-drug antibodies
that can compromise antigen binding, pharmacokinetics, safety
and/or efficacy241,242. Secondly, murine antibodies are typically
less efficient than their human counterparts in supporting effector
functions because of numerous differences between murine and
human immune systems243. Thirdly, murine antibodies typically
have a plasma half-life of only 1–2 days244 in patients — much shorter
than the ~21 day half-life for human IgG1 (ref. 245). A priori, this short
half-life is anticipated to limit target exposure and thereby efficacy of
murine antibodies. This pharmacokinetic difference is now known to
reflect the fact that murine IgG molecules bind much less efficiently
to the human recycling receptor, neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), than
do their human IgG counterparts246.
Multiple different technologies have been invented over
the past 40 years to mitigate the known limitations of murine
antibodies. For example, in chimerization of a murine antibody
the antigen-binding variable domains are incorporated into a
human antibody4,5. In humanization, the six antigen-binding
loops — complementarity-determining regions — within the
variable domains are commonly grafted from a murine antibody
into a human antibody6–8. A few additional murine residues,
such as Vernier zone247, are often also transferred to maintain
antigen-binding affinity. Additionally, many robust routes to human
antibodies have been developed since the 1990s as alternatives to
engineering murine antibodies. Technologies for human antibody
discovery include phage248, yeast249 and ribosome250 display
libraries; transgenic mice251,252, rats253,254, rabbits255 and chickens256;
and high-throughput antibody cloning from human B cells257.
Combinations of different technologies are increasingly being used
for antibody discovery, for example, ribosome display of antibody
fragment libraries in conjunction with deep sequencing250.

The number of approved ADCs — all in oncology to date — has
grown rapidly in recent years and now totals at least 16, excluding two
radioimmunoconjugates21 (Fig. 2e). Beyond calicheamicin, the cyto-
toxic payloads for approved ADCs exhibit several different mechanisms
of action that ultimately result in cell death21 (Fig. 2e). For example,
auristatins and maytansinoids inhibit tubulin polymerization, lead-
ing to mitotic arrest and apoptotic cell death. Pyrrolobenzodiazepine
dimers bind to the DNA minor grove, resulting in DNA alkylation, DNA
cross-linking and cell death. Camptothecins inhibit topoisomerase I,
which leads to DNA damage, cell cycle arrest and cell death. Future
trends in ADCs include the exploration of alternative small-molecule
payloads to increase their therapeutic index or enhance their drug-like
properties with applications that extend beyond oncology21. Payloads
being investigated include antibiotics37, immunomodulators, protein
degraders38,39, molecular glues39 and nucleic acids.
Bispecific antibodies
In 1960, the concept of bispecific antibodies was first demonstrated by
Nisonoff et al.40 using disulfide conjugation of Fab fragments obtained
by pepsin cleavage of rabbit IgG. In 2009 the first bispecific antibody,
catumaxomab (which targets epithelial cell adhesion molecule
(EpCAM) and CD3) was approved by the EMA for the treatment of malig-
nant ascites41. Catumaxomab was the first-in-class bispecific antibody
designed to recruit T cells to kill tumour cells; such drugs are now com-
monly known as T cell engagers (TCEs). Catumaxomab was removed
from the market for commercial reasons but then re-authorized in
Europe in 2025. The nearly 50-year lag between the advent of bispecific
antibodies40 and the approval of catumaxomab reflects decades of
enabling innovation, including the advent of hybridoma1 and hybrid
hybridoma42 technologies for the generation of monospecific and
bispecific antibodies, respectively. Additionally, basic research pro-
vided the conceptual breakthrough of using bispecific antibodies to
direct the killing of tumour cells by T cells43. The advent of efficient
biomanufacturing methods for bispecific antibodies was also crucial
to the clinical development and commercialization of these complex
and commonly multi-chain proteins44.
The number of approved bispecific antibodies has increased rap-
idly, particularly since 2020, and now totals at least 20 (Fig. 2f). These
are approved primarily for use in oncology23 and include 12 or more
TCEs. Bispecific antibodies are also approved for disease indications
in ophthalmology (faricimab-svoa, which targets VEGF and angiopoi-
etin 2), haematology (emicizumab-kxwh, which targets factor IXa
and factor X) and rheumatology (ozoralizumab). For most bispecific
antibodies, the two binding specificities are directly related to the
mechanism of action. In contrast, the trivalent VHH-based bispecific
antibody fragment ozoralizumab is bivalent for binding to TNF to sup-
port the mechanism of action and monovalent for binding to albumin
to extend the pharmacokinetic half-life.
The modular architecture of immunoglobulins has been used by
creative antibody engineers to design an impressively diverse array of
more than 100 different bispecific antibody formats45, to enable exqui-
site customization of properties for specific clinical settings. Tunable
features of bispecific antibodies include the number of binding sites
for each antigen (valency), their binding affinity and spatial geometry
for binding the two antigens, molecular weight, pharmacokinetics
and optional effector functions45. One useful classification scheme
for bispecific antibodies is their valency for each antigen-binding
specificity, for example, 1+1, 2+1 and 2+2 formats45,46 (Figs. 2f, 3 and 4).
A major protein engineering challenge with such bispecific antibod-
ies, now largely solved, has been to facilitate the preferential pair-
ing of cognate heavy and light chains and to promote heavy chain
heterodimerization for Fc-containing bispecific antibodies45. Antibody
technologies used in the efficient molecular assembly of currently
approved bispecific antibodies include BiTE47, knobs-into-holes48,49,
CrossMab50,51, common light chains52, DuoBody53 and Azymetric54.
Beyond bispecific antibodies many trispecific and multispecific anti-
bodies have advanced to clinical development22,55, although they have
not yet reached approval by health authorities.

Box 2 | Biomanufacturing of antibodies
The emergence of antibodies as therapeutics was enabled in
part by progress in biotechnology over nearly 50 years, including
the large-scale (kilogram to multi-tonne) recombinant production
of high-quality and meticulously characterized recombinant
proteins258,259. Almost all antibody therapeutics are manufactured in
either Chinese hamster ovary (CHO, ~73%) or myeloma (~22%) cell
lines (see Antibody Society data). Reliable mainstays of antibody
manufacturing for decades have been large volume (commonly
2,000–25,000 l) batch cell culture in stainless steel bioreactors and
chromatographic purification using protein A and other resins258,260.
Antibody titres in CHO cell cultures have increased from <1–2 g l−1
in the late 1990s to the 5–8 g l−1 that is routine today258 with titres of
>10 g l−1 achieved for some antibodies261. These large titre increases
reflect substantial improvements in CHO cell line development
and culture, for example, site-specific integration of expression
cassettes into the CHO host genome261, host cell line engineering262,
media optimization263, fed batch264 and high cell density culture,
including by perfusion265.
A major area of innovation in antibody biomanufacturing is
the advent of single-use equipment, particularly for cell culture
volumes of ≤2,000 l. Benefits of single-use technologies include
lower contamination risk, reduced cleaning validation and flexible
capacity266. Challenges of single-use biomanufacturing technologies
include scaling above 2,000 l, potential contamination from
leachable and extractable chemicals267 and waste generation266.
Sustainability benefits from single-use biomanufacturing are large
savings in water and energy needed to clean and sterilize stainless
steel facilities. Substantial progress in antibody biomanufacture
has also been made in continuous processing, leading to faster
production times and reduced cost of goods260. Multiple obstacles
to broader adoption of continuous processing include the need for
greater automation, real-time process monitoring, control systems
to optimize cell growth and more sophisticated process analytical
technologies266. The landscape of antibody biomanufacturing
continues to evolve with traditional batch processes operating
alongside emerging single-use technologies combined with
continuous processing268,269.

Monospecific IgG antibodies for B cell targeting in cancer
B cell malignancies are particularly tractable indications for antibody
therapies. This reflects that there are multiple surface targets that
are expressed predominantly on B cells and B cell malignancies, for
example, CD20, CD19, CD79 and CD22, and depletion of normal B cells
does not lead to dose-limiting toxicities. Before 1997, the mainstay
for treatment of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) was combination
chemotherapy: cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin hydrochloride, vin-
cristine sulfate and prednisone. In 1997, rituximab became the first
anti-CD20 antibody to be FDA-approved for the treatment of NHL56.
Rituximab is a chimeric antibody with variable domains derived from
the mouse anti-human CD20 antibody, IDEC-2B8, plus human κ light
chain and IgG1 constant heavy chain domains57. CD20 is predominantly
expressed on B-lineage cells, appearing first on pre-B cells, and then its
expression is extinguished as B cells mature into antibody-secreting
plasma cells. Selective targeting of B cell subsets with rituximab pre-
serves most pre-existing antibodies, particularly from long-lived
plasma cells that are CD20 negative. In haematological cancers,
CD20 is expressed on most malignant B cell leukaemias and lympho-
mas. Targeting of CD20 with rituximab has revolutionized the treat-
ment of multiple B cell malignancies, notably, follicular lymphoma
(FL), diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and chronic lymphocytic
leukaemia (CLL)56.
Rituximab eliminates CD20+ B cells through multiple mecha-
nisms — predominantly involving immune effector functions. These
include antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), through Fcγ
receptor interactions with natural killer cells, macrophages and neutro-
phils; antibody-mediated cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), also through
Fcγ receptors expressed on phagocytes; complement-mediated cyto-
toxicity (CDC); and direct apoptotic pathways. Two types of anti-CD20
antibody (types I and II) have been proposed on the basis of differences
in their ability to organize CD20 molecules into lipid rafts58. In addition,
type II anti-CD20 antibodies (obinutuzumab) can directly induce a
caspase-independent and lysosome-mediated cell death58. However,
the biophysical basis for these differences is not well understood.
Recent application of high-resolution imaging methods of type I (rituxi-
mab and ofatumumab) and type II (obinutuzumab) antibodies demon-
strate that both classes are capable of cross-linking and concatenating
CD20 on the Raji B cell line59. Additionally, live-cell imaging demon-
strates that rituximab, and to a much lesser degree obinutuzumab,
polarizes and accumulates antibody–CD20 complexes on microvilli,
accompanied by the formation of long microvilli protrusions. These
additional biophysical and cellular insights may provide an opportunity
for future generation of antibody therapeutics.
The clinical observation that patients with a higher affinity
polymorphism in FcγRIIIA (F158) and FcγRIIA (H131) have improved
responses with rituximab therapy60,61 provided a rationale to develop
obinutuzumab. Beyond directly inducing B cell cytotoxicity, obinu-
tuzumab is glyco-engineered to enhance ADCC. Specifically, obinu-
tuzumab is manufactured in a Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell line
overexpressing β1,4-N-acetylglucosamyltransferase III to increase
bisecting N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) on the glycan attached to the
Fc residue N297. This in turn decreases fucosylation at N297 and thereby
enhances binding to FcγRIIIA on effector cells62. Such glyco-engineering
to enhance ADCC and induction of cell death contributes to the benefit
observed with obinutuzumab in patients with rituximab-refractory
NHL and in the greater benefit for patients with CLL of obinutuzumab
in combination with chlorambucil when compared with rituximab
with chlorambucil63,64. However, obinutuzumab did not demonstrate
superiority in overall survival in untreated indolent NHL65 or in DLBCL66.
These differences in clinical outcome highlight how therapeutic effec-
tor mechanisms of action, even to the same target, need to be custom-
ized according to the underlying molecular causes of the disease to be
beneficial. Obinutuzumab is currently FDA-approved for treatment
of CLL and FL for patients who are refractory or relapsed (R/R) after
rituximab treatment and for patients with previously untreated stage II
bulky, stage III or stage IV FL67,68.
CD19, like CD20, is a targetable B cell lineage marker. CD19 is
more broadly expressed than CD20 on the surface of B-lineage cells:
pro-B cells, throughout B cell maturation and on most plasma cells.
Tafasitamab-cxix is a humanized anti-CD19 IgG1 with two Fc mutations
(S239D:I332E) to enhance ADCC and ADCP69 that is approved for treat-
ment of patients with R/R DLBCL70,71 (NCT05429268) in combination
with lenalidomide. The availability of multiple targets, such as CD19
or CD20, also provides patients with better therapeutic options, as
disease relapse is often associated with loss of antigen expression72.

Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is commonly observed with TCE
therapy because of T cell activation to kill the tumour cell. Hence, the
short half-life of blinatumomab was advantageous in the early days
of TCE development as treatment could be readily halted if patients
experienced CRS or other adverse events. This early benefit of short
half-life for TCEs was rendered less desirable by alternative treatment
innovations: corticosteroids and/or anti-IL-6 receptor antibody for
prophylaxis and/or treatment of CRS, step-dosing (smaller first dose
with higher subsequent doses) and s.c. dosing. Indeed, the field has
now shifted to TCEs with longer plasma half-lives, such as bispecific
IgG, and, less commonly, half-life-extended BiTEs, as exemplified by
tarlatamab73. This transition to TCEs with longer plasma half-lives eases
patient drug delivery burden with i.v. dosing frequencies commonly
every 3–4 weeks and s.c. dosing at home possible in some cases77.
Four TCEs targeting CD20 have been approved for treatment of
B cell lymphomas: mosunetuzumab for R/R FL78, glofitamab-gxbm
for R/R DLBCL and large B cell lymphoma79, epcoritamab-bysp for R/R
DLBCL80 and odronextamab for R/R FL81. The TCEs, teclistamab-cqyv82,
elranatamab-bcmm83 and linvoseltamab84, targeting B cell maturation
antigen (BCMA) — a crucial survival receptor for plasma cells — have
been approved for treatment of multiple myeloma. Also approved
for multiple myeloma is the TCE talquetamab-tgvs85, which targets
GPCR5D, a class C orphan G protein-coupled receptor. Most TCEs
(six of seven) approved for targeting B cell malignancies have a 1+1
bispecific IgG format (Fig. 3). In contrast, glofitamab-gxbm has a novel
2+1 CrossMab51 structure in which an anti-CD3 Fab is genetically fused
to the ‘bottom’ one of anti-CD20 Fab and a second anti-CD20 Fab is
present on the other arm to provide bivalency for CD20 (Fig. 3). This
2+1 valency for CD20 and CD3 provides superior avidity for CD20 over
the standard 1+1 valency that translates into greater potency86.
A recent extension of antibody engineering is for cell-based thera-
peutics, notably CAR T cells. Unlike the antibody therapeutics dis-
cussed above, this platform involves a molecularly engineered CAR,
consisting of an antibody fragment (scFv or biparatopic VHH fragment)
fused to a transmembrane domain and an intracellular domain that
typically includes the signalling domains from either CD28 or CD137
(4-1BB) and the CD3ζ chain of the TCR, into a patient’s autologous
T cells87. These cells are then expanded in vitro and re-infused into
patients. The scFv binds to a tumour antigen — CD19 for B cell malig-
nancies and BCMA for multiple myeloma — to induce T cell activation
and killing of the tumour. Five CAR T cell therapies targeting CD19 are
approved by the FDA for the treatment of various B cell lymphomas:
axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel, brexucabtagene autoleucel,
obecabtagene autoleucel and lisocabtagene maraleucel. Additionally,
two CAR T cells targeting BCMA are approved by the FDA for treatment
of multiple myeloma: ciltacabtagene autoleucel and idecabtagene
vicleucel88,89. Unlike protein-based antibody therapeutics, CAR T cells
are living drugs that proliferate and dynamically evolve into other cell
states over time90. This is an exciting convergence between protein
and cellular biotherapeutics about which there is much still to be learnt
in the future.
Orthogonally to these antibody and scFv-based platforms, anti-
body conjugates have also transformed the treatment of haemato-
logical malignancies. Radioimmunoconjugates were among the first
antibodies with payloads approved for therapeutic use. Ibritumomab
tiuxetan conjugated to yttrium-90 and [131I]tositumomab were both
approved by the FDA for treatment of B cell malignancies91,92. However,
they have had limited clinical and commercial impact, reflecting their
high complexity of manufacture, complicated clinical administration
and the development of simpler and more efficacious B cell-targeted
therapies.
Since the approval of the first ADC, gemtuzumab ozogamicin
(discussed above), five ADCs have been approved for treatment of B cell
lymphomas, leukaemias and multiple myeloma (Fig. 3). These ADCs
target different antigens with diverse payloads: CD30 (monomethyl
auristatin E), CD22 (calicheamicin), CD79b (monomethyl auristatin E),
CD19 (pyrrolobenzodiazepine dimer) and BCMA (monomethyl aurista-
tin F). Inotuzumab ozogamicin — the second ADC armed with cali-
cheamicin — targets CD22, which is expressed on normal and malignant
B cells, as well as on plasmacytoid dendritic cells, and is approved for
treatment of R/R CD22-positive B cell precursor ALL93. Polatuzumab
vedotin-piiq is an ADC consisting of a humanized anti-CD79b antibody
conjugated via a protease-cleavable linker to monomethyl auristatin E,
a microtubule inhibitor. CD79b is a transmembrane signalling subunit
of the B cell antigen receptor and is rapidly internalized following
binding at the cell surface. Polatuzumab vedotin-piiq is approved
for treatment of newly diagnosed large B cell lymphoma in combina-
tion with rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin and prednisone
(pola-R-CHP)94. Loncastuximab tesirine-lpyl is an ADC consisting of a
humanized anti-CD19 antibody conjugated to the pyrrolobenzodiaz-
epine dimer, SG3199 (ref. 95). In contrast to auristatins, pyrrolobenzo-
diazepine dimers are inter-strand minor groove DNA cross-linkers that
have the advantage of not distorting native DNA structures, thereby
evading DNA repair mechanisms. Loncastuximab tesirine-lpyl is
approved for treatment of R/R DLBCL95. Belantamab mafodotin-blmf
is an ADC consisting of a humanized antibody targeting BCMA, glyco-
engineered (afucosylated) Fc for enhanced ADCC, conjugated to mono-
methyl auristatin F via a non-cleavable maleimidocaproyl linker96.

In summary, major advances in antibody design over the past
30 years now provide patients with B cell malignancies with multiple
therapeutic options, notably monospecific antibodies, bispecific anti-
bodies such as TCEs, as well as ADCs and CAR T cells (Fig. 3). The target
antigens on B cells described here are lineage markers rather than onco-
genic drivers. Consequently, loss of antigen expression and upregula-
tion of drug transporters, as well as other resistance mechanisms, often
cause disease relapse99. However, the availability of distinct therapeutic
antigens in conjunction with diverse antibody therapeutics with mul-
tiple mechanisms of action provide many options to extend the lives
of patients and potentially cure them of their disease, perhaps even
without cytotoxic chemotherapy.
B cell targeting in immune-mediated diseases
One of the first randomized controlled studies demonstrating clini-
cal efficacy of B cell-modulating therapies was the use of rituximab
in rheumatoid arthritis in 2004 (ref. 100). These early observations
were subsequently confirmed in phase III studies that led to FDA
approval for rituximab in patients with rheumatoid arthritis refrac-
tory to anti-TNF therapies101. During the following 12 years, rituximab
was demonstrated to be efficacious and was approved by the FDA to
treat patients with granulomatosis with polyangiitis (2011), micro-
scopic polyangiitis (2011) and pemphigus vulgaris (2018)102,103. The
efficacy of rituximab in these B cell-mediated diseases revealed multiple
non-mutually exclusive mechanisms by which B cells contribute to
disease pathogenesis, including autoantibody-mediated cytotoxic-
ity, immune complex-mediated end organ damage, amplification of
immune activation through cytokine secretion and co-stimulation and
tertiary lymphoid structure formation104.
Conversely, rituximab was not efficacious in phase III clinical
trials of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), lupus nephritis and
primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS)105,106. Retrospective
analysis of the phase III LUNAR study of rituximab in lupus nephritis
demonstrated substantial variability in peripheral B cell depletion
and that achievement of complete and prolonged depletion of CD19+
B cells was associated with complete clinical responses107. Of note,

In multiple sclerosis, rituximab provided substantial reduction
in gadolinium-enhancing lesions and clinical relapses in a phase II
clinical trial of patients with relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis
(RRMS)112. However, rituximab was not efficacious in a phase III clinical
trial of PPMS113. Anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) were detected in 26–37%
of patients with multiple sclerosis following treatment with rituximab,
although the presence of ADAs was not associated with any clinical
adverse events114. This immunogenicity of rituximab likely reflects that
it is a chimeric antibody with mouse variable domains and that patients
with RRMS or PPMS do not receive concomitant immunosuppression,
unlike patients with rheumatoid arthritis. Although safe for almost all
patients treated, there are rare cases of rituximab-induced serum sick-
ness in multiple sclerosis115. However, there are no predictive markers
to identify before therapy patients who may develop these rare adverse
events. In SLE, high rates of ADAs to rituximab have also been reported
and are associated with earlier drug elimination116. Ocrelizumab and
ofatumumab are humanized and human anti-CD20 antibodies, respec-
tively, that have been approved by the FDA for treatment of RRMS117,118.
Ocrelizumab is also approved by the FDA for treatment of PPMS119. Thus,
humanized and human antibodies can decrease the potential for ADAs,
their potential negative impact on drug clearance and development of
rare type III hypersensitivity reactions.
Bispecific antibodies to CD19 and CD20 are being tested in SLE and
lupus nephritis (NCT05835986 and NCT05155345) and are anticipated
to provide a better understanding of whether these more potent B cell-
modulating therapies can deliver greater clinical efficacy. An excit-
ing recent development is the emergence of CAR T cell therapy for
treatment of autoimmune disorders, including SLE, lupus nephritis,
inflammatory myositis and multiple sclerosis120,121. We await larger
clinical trials of these new scFv-directed cell therapies against CD19,
BCMA and other B cell lineage markers.
HER2 targeting for breast cancer
At least 11 different antibody therapeutics have been approved for
use in oncology for various tumours overexpressing the HER2 proto-
oncogene, including breast, gastric, biliary tract, gastro-oesophageal
cancers and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (Fig. 4). Here, we focus
on how advances in antibody engineering have improved clinical out-
comes in the treatment of HER2+ breast cancer. The overexpression
of HER2 as an oncogenic driver in ~20% of breast cancers provided a
rationale for the development of antibody therapeutics, the first of
which was trastuzumab122.
The humanized IgG1 antibody trastuzumab binds to subdo-
main IV of the HER2 extracellular domain (ECD), inhibits tumour cell
proliferation and supports ADCC123. Additionally, trastuzumab shows
additivity or synergy with cytotoxic chemotherapy124. Trastuzumab
in combination with chemotherapy improved both progression-free
and overall survival in HER2+ metastatic breast cancer125. In 1998 tras-
tuzumab was approved for the treatment of HER2+ metastatic breast
cancer. Trastuzumab was accompanied by the first ever FDA-approved
companion diagnostic test (HercepTest) for detection of HER2 protein
expression by immunohistochemistry126. Subsequent clinical trials
led to the approval of trastuzumab for the treatment of early HER2+
breast cancer127.
The humanized IgG1 antibody pertuzumab binds to subdomain II
of the HER2 ECD and prevents heterodimerization of HER2 with other
HER family members, namely, EGFR, HER3 and HER4. The combina-
tion of trastuzumab and pertuzumab results in enhanced inhibition
of downstream signalling128. Clinical trials with trastuzumab plus per-
tuzumab administered by i.v. infusion in combination with chem-
otherapy, demonstrated greater clinical benefit than trastuzumab
with chemotherapy across the spectrum of metastatic, adjuvant and
neoadjuvant HER2+ breast cancers129–131. A further advance was cofor-
mulation of trastuzumab with pertuzumab plus hyaluronidase-zzxf,
thereby enabling s.c. delivery for treatment of HER2+ breast cancer in
conjunction with chemotherapy132.

Before trastuzumab, cytotoxic chemotherapies and surgery were
the mainstay of treatment of HER2+ breast cancer. Whereas surgery
remains a mainstay for therapy in early disease, chemotherapeutic
regimens have a narrow therapeutic index that limits their potential
efficacy. The first ADC introduced for the treatment of HER2+ breast
cancer was ado-trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1). This ADC consists
of trastuzumab (T) covalently connected via a non-cleavable linker to
a maytansinoid, DM1, that binds to microtubules, leading to mitotic
disruption and apoptosis in HER2-overexpressing cell lines139. After
binding of cell surface HER2, T-DM1 is internalized through the endolys-
osomal pathway and undergoes degradation, releasing its cytotoxic
payload to kill the tumour cell. T-DM1 prolonged both progression-free
and overall survival and was less toxic and better tolerated by patients
when compared with lapatinib and capecitabine in patients with HER2+
breast cancer previously treated with trastuzumab and taxanes140.
T-DM1 is also approved to treat recurrent disease after neoadjuvant
therapy141.
ADC linker design and DAR impact the amount of payload deliv-
ered per tumour, as well as in vivo ADC stability, pharmacokinetics,
efficacy and toxicity. Additionally, cell permeability of the released
payload is likely necessary for efficient killing of bystander cells.
T-DM1 has a mean DAR of ~3.5. Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan-nxki
(T-DXd) is a second-generation anti-HER2 ADC with a higher mean
DAR (~8). In T-DXd, trastuzumab is conjugated through a cleav-
able and self-immolative amino methylene spacer to deruxtecan
(DXd)142. DXd inhibits DNA topoisomerase I and thereby DNA repli-
cation resulting in cell death. T-DXd (previously known as DS-8201a)
releases cell-permeable metabolites and exhibits bystander cell kill-
ing activity143, whereas T-DM1 releases cell-impermeable metabo-
lites and does not139. T-DXd was initially approved for treatment of
unresectable or metastatic HER2+ breast cancer144,145. Surprisingly
and importantly, T-DXd is also efficacious in metastatic breast can-
cers that express low levels of HER2 (ref. 146). Efficacy in this lat-
ter population may reflect a higher dose (5.4 mg kg−1 every 3 weeks
versus 3.6 mg kg−1 every 3 weeks for T-DM1), higher DAR and potential
bystander effect to overcome HER2 expression tumour heterogeneity.
Subsequent approvals for T-DXd include HER2-low and HER2-ultralow
unresectable or metastatic breast cancer, as well as HER2+ metastatic
or recurrent breast cancer in the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting147.
T-DXd is also approved for other tumour types, namely, NSCLC with
an activating HER2 mutation and trastuzumab-experienced HER2+
gastric or gastro-oesophageal cancers147. Another HER2-directed
ADC, distamab vedotin, with a monomethyl auristatin E payload,
was recently approved in China for treatment of HER2+ gastric cancer
(see Antibody Society data).
Multiple additional HER2-targeted ADCs with diverse payloads
and linker types are presently in development that will provide addi-
tional insights into how mechanistic differences may improve efficacy
and minimize toxicities122. In addition, further understanding of the
impact of ADCs on direct and indirect tumour cytotoxicity, the spatial
distribution of HER2-high versus HER2-low targeted tumours and the
potential impact of the stromal and immune compartments will also
likely be important not only for next-generation drug design, but also
for combination therapies that can modulate innate and acquired
resistance mechanisms. Multiple investigational therapeutics are being
explored to test these concepts. The bispecific antibody IBI315 (target-
ing HER2 and PD1) enhances antitumour activity in preclinical studies
of HER2+ gastric cancer and reached clinical trials for this indication
(NCT04162327 and NCT05608785). Cinrebafusp alfa (PRS-343), is a
bispecific fusion protein that combines a 4-1BB-targeting Anticalin
and a HER2-targeting antibody. Cinrebafusp alfa demonstrated some
antitumour activity in a phase I study and is currently in a phase II trial
(NCT05190445) in HER2-low gastric and gastro-oesophageal junction
adenocarcinoma. MT-5111 is an engineered toxin body: scFv targeting
HER2 genetically fused to the enzymatically active de-immunized,
ribosome-inactivating cytotoxic payload Shiga-like toxin A subu-
nit. MT-5111 reached phase I clinical trial in HER2+ solid tumours
(NCT04029922).
One biparatopic antibody (zanidatamab-hrii) and one bispecific
antibody (zenocutuzumab-zbco, targeting HER2 and HER3) have been
approved to target HER2+ cancers (Fig. 4). Zanidatamab-hrii binds
to two distinct epitopes on subdomains II and IV of the HER2 ECD,
thereby inhibiting HER2 homodimerization and heterodimerization
with EGFR, HER3 or HER4 and downstream intracellular signalling148.
Additionally, zanidatamab-hrii facilitates HER2 internalization and
subsequent downregulation, leading to a reduction of HER2 recep-
tor on the surface of tumour cells. Zanidatamab-hrii also induces
tumour cell killing via CDC, ADCC and ADCP. These mechanisms of
action result in tumour growth inhibition and cell death in vitro and
in vivo. Zanidatamab-hrii demonstrated clinical efficacy in multiple
HER2+ tumour types, including biliary tract cancer, and is currently
approved for this indication149,150.
Chimeric proteins resulting from neuregulin 1 (NRG1) genetic
fusions are rare (~0.2% prevalence) oncogenic drivers in some solid
tumours, including NSCLC and pancreatic adenocarcinoma151. NRG1
fusions facilitate heterodimerization of HER3 with HER2, trigger-
ing cellular proliferation and oncogenic activity152. Zenocutuzumab-
zbco is a common light chain52 bispecific antibody (to HER2 and HER3)
that blocks NRG1 binding to HER3 and prevents heterodimerization
with HER2. Zenocutuzumab demonstrated efficacy in a registrational
phase II trial (eNRGy) in patients with advanced NRG1 fusion-positive
cancer such as NSCLC and pancreatic cancer153, leading to approval
for these indications.
Just as discussed above for haematological cancers, TCEs and CAR
T cells are also being explored for HER2+ malignancies. Indeed, there
are 20 active clinical trials for HER2 CAR T cells as of May 2025 (https://
www.clinicaltrials.gov). Unlike CD19, CD20 and BCMA, which are
expressed almost exclusively on immune cells, HER2 is also expressed at
lower levels on normal epithelial cells154, as well as on cardiomyocytes155.
Treatment with trastuzumab is associated with a risk of cardiotoxicity,
which is greatest in patients receiving concomitant anthracyclines156.
As technological advances increase the potency of antibody therapeu-
tics, one must be watchful for adverse events associated with HER2
expression on normal tissue. For example, the death of one patient in
a clinical trial with a HER2 CAR T cell apparently resulting from CRS
has been reported157. Avidity engineering provides a way to enhance
the selectivity of TCEs, and potentially CAR T cells too, for efficient
killing of tumour cells expressing high levels of cognate antigen while
sparing normal cells expressing lower levels of antigen158. For example,
a HER2-targeting TCE was designed with 2+1 bispecific IgG format
using two low-affinity HER2-binding arms. This avidity-engineered
TCE efficiently killed tumour cells expressing high levels of HER2 with
attenuated killing of cells expressing low levels of HER2. Such avid-
ity engineering has the potential to reduce the adverse events from

[[page:13]]
TCEs and increase the therapeutic index. Runimotamab, a HER2-tar-
geting TCE that is engineered for avidity, is currently in a phase Ia/Ib
clinical trial (NCT03448042) for HER2-expressing cancers. Further
advances in logic-gated antibodies159 that can distinguish between
normal and cancer cells may further increase the therapeutic index of
next-generation therapies.
Current practices and future trends for
antibody delivery
As noted previously, most antibodies are administered by the i.v. or s.c.
route (Fig. 2d). Below we discuss the strengths and limitations of i.v.
and s.c. delivery, alongside opportunities to mitigate these limitations.
Strengths and limitations of intravenous antibody delivery
An attractive feature of i.v. delivery is its broad applicability for
antibodies for many different clinical indications. Additionally, the
plasma bioavailability following i.v. delivery is 100% by definition. How-
ever, a drawback of i.v. infusion is that treatment commonly requires
at least 1 h and often multiple hours in an outpatient infusion centre
or hospital, adding to the cost and inconvenience for patients160.
These multiple burdens associated with the i.v. delivery of antibodies
can decrease compliance and reduce patient access to treatment as
observed during the COVID-19 pandemic161.
Antibody engineering can partially mitigate the burdens of i.v.
delivery of antibodies by enabling longer intervals between doses.
This concept is illustrated by the iterative design of antibodies target-
ing the complement component, C5, for chronic treatment of the
haematological disorder paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria.
The humanized anti-C5 antibody eculizumab162 has a terminal
plasma half-life of 11–17 days, requiring frequent i.v. administration.
A representative treatment regimen for eculizumab is weekly dos-
ing for 5 weeks followed by maintenance therapy every 2 weeks163.
Ravulizumab-cwvz (previously known as ALXN-1210)164 is an engi-
neered version of eculizumab with two half-life-extending modifica-
tions. First, ravulizumab-cwvz has pH-dependent antigen binding that
allows recycling (Fig. 5) within cells to mitigate target-mediated drug
disposition. Second, ravulizumab-cwvz incorporates two Fc mutations
(M428L:N434S)165 to increase recycling efficiency by the immunoglobu-
lin salvage receptor, neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). Ravulizumab-cwvz
has an extended terminal plasma half-life of 50–64 days in patients,
allowing infrequent i.v. dosing, for example, a single loading dose
followed by maintenance doses every 4–8 weeks166.

Strengths and limitations of subcutaneous delivery
S.c. injection has multiple advantages over i.v. infusion, including
much faster dosing (typically less than 10 min) the possibility of home
administration, greater patient convenience and compliance, and lower
health-care costs160,167. Physical limitations of s.c. injection of antibodies
typically include small dosing volumes (0.5–2 ml per injection site) and
required high dosing concentrations (100–200 mg ml−1)168. Achieving
high antibody concentration is often challenging owing to antibody
self-interaction, which can result in high viscosity, insufficient solubil-
ity, aggregation, gelation and opalescence169. An additional limitation
of s.c. delivery is that the bioavailability of approved antibodies varies
more than twofold (49–96%)10. Low bioavailability may undermine
the feasibility of achieving the target dose required for s.c. delivery,
as well as increasing the cost of goods. Moreover, the bioavailability
of antibodies in humans is not yet routinely predictable preclinically
using either computational or experimental methods, prompting an
open challenge to develop better approaches170.
Immunogenicity, including the elicitation of ADAs, is a potential
risk for all protein therapeutics, including antibodies12,171. ADAs on
rare occasions are clinically significant in that they adversely affect the
pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, safety or efficacy of protein
therapeutics12,172. Systematic comparison of the immunogenicity of
protein therapeutics (n = 20) revealed that the incidence of ADAs in
most cases (70%) was similar following i.v. or s.c. delivery. However, the
incidence of ADAs was slightly higher in a few examples for s.c. injection
(20% of cases) and substantially higher in rare instances (10% of cases)160.
Given these small differences in observed ADA rates, immunogenicity
is not commonly a major consideration in deciding between i.v. and s.c.
administration for antibody and other protein therapeutics.
The plasma pharmacokinetics of antibodies are inevitably differ-
ent following i.v. or s.c. administration160. For example, the maximal
plasma concentration (Cmax) will be lower after s.c. injection. Further-
more, tmax (the time to reach Cmax) is much longer, taking multiple days
compared with hours173. These pharmacokinetic differences for s.c.
delivery can be either an advantage or disadvantage depending upon
the circumstances160. On the positive side, a lower Cmax after s.c. injec-
tion may lower the risk of CRS for TCEs160. A downside is the difficulty
in monitoring and managing infusion-related reactions that are often
more common and severe for the first dose, especially if these reactions
occur days after a s.c. injection. This safety concern can be mitigated by
i.v. infusion for the first dose followed by s.c. injection for subsequent
doses, as exemplified by approved dosing regimens for several differ-
ent antibodies, including caplacizumab-yhdp, mirikizumab-mrkz,
pozelimab-bbfg and crovalimab-akkz.
Multiple complementary antibody engineering and formulation
strategies are possible to enhance the potential of antibodies for s.c.
delivery (Table 1). For example, coformulation of antibodies with hya-
luronidase to hydrolyse hyaluronic acid in connective tissue enables
s.c. injection volumes to be increased from 2 ml to at least 15 ml, as
demonstrated for at least six approved antibody therapeutics (Table 1).
In 1998, trastuzumab, delivered by i.v. infusion, was approved for HER2+
metastatic breast cancer. In 2013, trastuzumab coformulated with
hyaluronidase-oysk and delivered by s.c. injection was approved for
HER2+ early breast cancer (Fig. 4). Furthermore, antibody engineering
can be used to improve antibody high-concentration properties such as
reducing viscosity174–177, thereby facilitating s.c. delivery. Additionally,
engineering can be used to increase the pharmacodynamic effect of
antibodies and thereby lower the required antibody dose (Table 1). For
example, engineering antibodies with sweeping178,179 or recycling180,181
properties (Fig. 5) enables super-stoichiometric neutralization of the
target antigen by the antibody. The anti-C5 antibody, crovalimab-akkz
(previously known as SKY-59), was engineered for recycling181,182 and
is approved for the treatment of paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglo-
binuria by i.v. infusion for the first dose followed by s.c. injection for
subsequent doses.
Future opportunities for antibody therapeutics
This Review focuses on the 50-year odyssey from the advent of hybri-
doma technology in 1975 (ref. 1) to the present era of antibody therapeu-
tics (Fig. 2) and their clinical application to targeting B cells (Fig. 3) and
HER2+ cancers (Fig. 4). Emerging trends for antibody therapeutics with
broad potential impact have been extensively reviewed elsewhere10,13,
including for bispecifics22,23 and ADCs21. Here, we opine briefly on four
major areas of innovation that hold much promise for future antibody
therapeutics: first, applications of artificial intelligence to antibody
design; second, conditional antigen-binding activity to enhance the
therapeutic index of anticancer antibodies; third, exploration of immu-
noglobulin isotypes beyond IgG; and fourth, more facile generation
of agonist antibodies. Finally, we comment on a few grand challenges
with antibodies for which even partial solutions may have profound
benefit for next-generation antibody therapeutics: efficient delivery
of antibodies into the central nervous system; engineering antibodies
for oral administration; and engineering antibodies with low risk for
immunogenicity in patients.
Applications of artificial intelligence to antibody design
The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry183 was awarded to David Baker for
“computational protein design” jointly with John Jumper and Demis Has-
sabis for “protein structure prediction” with AlphaFold184. This Nobel
Prize recognizes the enormous potential of artificial intelligence tools
for protein sciences, including the design of antibody and other protein
therapeutics. Indeed, computational design can endow proteins with
novel functionality that can be difficult to accomplish using rational
engineering or directed evolution. This is highlighted by the de novo
design of high-affinity (picomolar range) protein binders reported by
David Baker and collaborators185, as well as the computational design
of zinc finger domains to customize DNA-binding motifs186.
However, there are many steps beyond the design of novel antibod-
ies and other proteins to ensure that they have drug-like properties.
Indeed, the design of antibody therapeutics is quintessentially one of
optimization across multiple desired attributes. First, one needs the
desired binding specificity, affinity, biological activities plus phar-
macokinetics. Additionally, favourable developability characteris-
tics are desirable, including chemical and thermal stability. Desirable
attributes for s.c. delivery include favourable high-concentration
properties, high bioavailability and long-term (multi-month) stability
at ambient temperatures. Low risk for immunogenicity is also a valuable
attribute for engineered protein and antibody therapeutics12. Anti-
body engineers can finely tune these multiple properties to produce
molecules with drug-like properties with great success, albeit using
resource-intensive methods10.
The computational design and optimization of antibod-
ies for affinity187–189, developability190,191, including low viscosity192,
humanization193 and immunogenicity194, is already under way, and
the de novo design of VHH antibodies has also been reported195. The
computational methods that underlie these breakthroughs con-
tinues to evolve, as reviewed elsewhere196. However, the ultimate
promise of computational antibody design is integration of all of the

[[page:15]]
aforementioned properties into an antibody from conception — in
silico multi-parameter optimization. Artificial intelligence methods
have the potential to generate or optimize antibodies in ways that are
difficult or not possible with extant experimental approaches, as well
as reducing the time and/or cost to generate clinical candidates197.
Engineering antibodies with conditional antigen binding
Antibody therapeutics have grown in their impact in oncology over
nearly three decades as illustrated in this Review with multiple genera-
tions of antibodies targeting B cells and HER2. Much current research
is focused on increasing efficacy and reducing toxicity for anticancer
antibodies11, including engineering antibodies for conditional anti-
gen binding. For example, antibody prodrugs (Probody) have been
generated by fusion of antibody variable domains with peptides to
attenuate antigen binding198. Selective activation of such antibody
prodrugs by tumour-associated proteases has the potential to enhance
the selectivity of antibodies for tumours over normal tissue and thereby
increase the therapeutic index11. Alternative strategies for engineering
antibodies for enhanced binding in tumours include exploiting the
slightly acidic pH in the tumour microenvironment180,199, or binding to
extracellular ATP200,201 or calcium ions202. Clinical testing of the potential
benefits of conditional antigen binding by antibodies is in progress
(for example, NCT06265688 and IRAS ID 270978).
Immunoglobulin isotypes
A large proportion of approved antibodies have human IgG1 (57%), IgG2
(7%) or IgG4 (22%) (see Antibody Society data) heavy chain constant
domains. The Fc region of IgG therapeutics is commonly engineered to
modify one or more functions, as comprehensively reviewed by Wilkin-
son and Hale9. The common selection of the human IgG1 isotype for
antibody therapeutics likely reflects in part the ease with which IgG1
Fc functions have been successfully tailored for specific clinical appli-
cations, including enhancement or attenuation of effector functions,
extension or reduction of plasma half-life, heterodimerization to make
bispecific antibodies, ablation of protein A binding and enablement of
site-specific conjugation for ADCs (see Antibody Society data)9. There
are no approved IgG3 therapeutics, suggesting that the advantages of
this isotype are outweighed by potential limitations. Proposed advan-
tages for IgG3 include high affinity for activating Fcγ receptors, effec-
tive complement fixation and a long hinge that may improve suitability
for low-abundance targets203. Potential downsides for IgG3 include
rapid degradation, short plasma half-life and immunogenicity risk
stemming from multiple polymorphisms (allotypes)203.
Recent years have seen growing interest in evaluation of other
human immunoglobulin isotypes in the clinic, including IgM204,205,
IgA206 and IgE207,208. These alternative immunoglobulin isotypes war-
rant exploration as they have distinctly different properties from IgG,
including their avidity, effector function profile and biodistribution in
human as illustrated here with IgM, which is reviewed in greater detail
elsewhere204,205. IgM forms pentamers or alternatively hexamers with
10 or 12 antigen-binding sites, respectively, compared with only two
antigen-binding sites for IgG. The large number of binding sites for
IgM can be advantageous over IgG in supporting high-avidity antigen
binding, target clustering and very efficient complement activation.
For example, reformatting an anti-SARS-CoV-2 spike protein IgG into an
IgM pentamer increased the potency for neutralization of SARS-CoV-2
in vitro 230-fold (ref. 209). Major challenges to development of IgM
as therapeutics, include their large size (900–950 kDa for pentamers
and 1,050–1,150 kDa for hexamers), lack of protein A binding, limited
stability and short pharmacokinetic half-life in human. Although a
human IgM isotype antibody, nebacumab (anti-endotoxin) (Fig. 2c)
was approved in several European countries for treatment of sepsis
(1991), it was quickly withdrawn from the market because of lack of
efficacy and increased mortality210.
An alternative strategy to the direct development of IgM, IgA and
IgE as therapeutics is to engineer IgG to endow it with properties from
other immunoglobulin isotypes. For example, the HexaBody platform
for hexamerization of IgG was designed to mimic IgM211. A HexaBody
targeting CD38 (GEN3014) is in a phase I/II trial for R/R haematological
malignancies (NCT04824794). A HexaBody targeting OX40 (GEN1055)
is in a phase I/II trial for malignant solid tumours as a monotherapy
and in combination with pembrolizumab (NCT06391775). Another
approach to expanding the functions of IgG is to design hybrids with
other isotypes. For example, a hybrid IgE–IgG1 (IgEG) of trastuzumab
was recently engineered to combine the functions of IgG1 and IgE212.
In vitro trastuzumab IgEG showed a broader range of immune functions
than either corresponding IgG1 or IgE. In mice the pharmacokinetic
half-life of trastuzumab IgEG (3.4 days) was intermediate between
that of the corresponding IgG1 (14.1 days) and that of IgE (0.008 days),
whereas the antitumour activity of the IgEG was comparable to that of
both IgE and IgG1 versions212.
Agonist antibodies
Antibody therapeutics binding to either receptors or their cog-
nate ligands are well established as antagonists of receptor–ligand
interactions213. In contrast, the development of agonist antibodies for
activation of receptors214,215, including cytokine mimetic antibodies216,
is at a much earlier stage, reflecting that they can be challenging to
identify. Cytokine mimetic antibodies have both potential advantages
and limitations as therapeutics compared with engineered cytokines217.
Advantages of cytokine mimetic antibodies include typically more
favourable developability properties, potential for new biological
properties, avoidance of natural inhibitors and readily tunable phar-
macokinetic properties. Additionally, any ADAs elicited by the cytokine
mimetic antibodies are unlikely to neutralize endogenous cytokines.
Disadvantages of cytokine mimetic antibodies are that they can be
more difficult to design or discover and they may not recapitulate all
of the desired natural biological properties of the cytokine.
Delivery of antibodies to the central nervous system
Beyond enhancing antibodies for s.c. administration, delivery to organs
and tissues, including the central nervous system218, is a major research
focus with antibodies with substantial therapeutic potential10. For
example, engineering antibodies with bispecificity, such as binding
to a target antigen and to the transferrin receptor (TfR), can enhance
uptake across the blood–brain barrier into the brain several fold as
demonstrated in non-human primates219. Trontinemab, a 2+1 bispe-
cific antibody (targeting amyloid-β and TfR)220 that is also known as
a Brainshuttle, reduced amyloid plaques in the brain in a phase Ib/IIa
clinical trial in Alzheimer disease (NCT04639050) with phase III trials
now being planned. Potential future applications of bispecific antibod-
ies with enhanced delivery to the brain include the treatment of brain
cancers or other cancers that have metastasized to the brain.
Clinical proof of concept for the use of TfR to enhance uptake
into the central nervous system has been achieved in the context of
enzyme replacement therapy. Mucopolysaccharidosis type II (Hunter
syndrome) is a rare X-linked recessive lysosomal storage disease caused
by a deficiency of iduronate 2-sulfatase that leads to a build-up of

[[page:16]]
glycosaminoglycans in cells, resulting in various symptoms and com-
plications. Pabinafusp alfa is a blood–brain barrier penetrant fusion
protein consisting of an antibody that binds to TfR fused to iduro-
nate 2-sulfatase221. Systemic administration of pabinafusp alfa utilizes
TfR-mediated transcytosis to increase iduronate 2-sulfatase levels in
the central nervous system. In 2021, pabinafusp alfa was approved in
Japan to treat mucopolysaccharidosis type II. This approach is likely to
be used for other enzyme replacement therapies that require central
nervous system penetration.
Oral delivery of antibodies
The oral delivery of antibodies and other proteins is a long-sought-after
pharmaceutical goal to increase patient compliance and convenience
by circumventing the discomfort associated with parenteral delivery
methods and by minimizing related toxicities222,223. However, oral deliv-
ery of proteins is fraught with substantial obstacles, including inactiva-
tion of proteins by low pH and proteases, as well as inefficient antibody
release at the desired site in the gastrointestinal tract. Further and com-
pounding challenges include therapeutic doses limited by pill burden
and substantial permeability barriers to uptake from the gastrointesti-
nal tract into systemic circulation, predominantly involving mucus and
epithelia. Steps towards enabling the oral delivery of proteins include
the use of nanoparticles, permeation enhancers, hydrogels, micronee-
dles, microemulsion, protease inhibitors and cell-penetrating peptides,
as reviewed extensively elsewhere222,223. For example, sophisticated and
ingestible medical devices have been used preclinically for systemic
delivery of antibodies, peptides and small molecules (adalimumab,
GLP1 analogue, insulin and adrenaline) by auto-injection through the
gut mucosa224,225. At least 13 different protein and peptide therapeutics,
albeit no antibodies yet, are approved for oral delivery10, including
semaglutide, cyclosporine, desmopressin acetate and octreotide223.
Recently a VHH antibody targeting IL-23 receptor was engineered for
high affinity and protease stability for oral delivery226. This anti-IL-23
receptor VHH was comparably efficacious in mouse models of colitis
following oral gavage to a systemically administered anti-IL-23 receptor
IgG. In cynomolgus monkeys, a single pill of encapsulated VHH gave
sustained IL-23 receptor inhibition in the peripheral blood for at least
24 hours226. Nevertheless, further progress is needed to realize the
full potential of oral delivery for transformative antibody and other
protein therapeutics.
Engineering antibodies with low risk for immunogenicity
A major focus in the early years of antibody therapeutics was in over-
coming the limitations of murine antibodies in patients, including
the immunogenicity of these foreign proteins (Box 1). In recent years,
immunogenicity risk assessment and mitigation have emerged as
urgent requirements in the design and optimization of antibody
and other protein therapeutics12,171,227. A plethora of powerful pro-
tein engineering methods now exist to improve the existing proper-
ties of antibodies or to endow them with new functions unknown to
nature10. Unfortunately, the potential for such extensive engineering
of antibodies to elicit ADAs that compromise safety and/or efficacy
is poorly understood. For some investigational antibodies, such as
bococizumab228, a high incidence of ADAs was a major factor in the
decision to terminate further clinical development. Immunogenicity
mitigation strategies include engineering out T cell epitopes229 and
improving biophysical properties as exemplified by bococizumab230.
In contrast to the highly immunogenic antibody bococizumab,
the extensively engineered bispecific antibody emicizumab-kxwh
(mentioned above) had a desirably low incidence of ADAs (5.1%) in
phase III clinical trials231. This low ADA rate may reflect in part the use
of a common light chain52 in the design of emicizumab and/or T cell
epitope identification and removal232. Emicizumab demonstrates that
minimization of the incidence of ADAs in the clinic can be achieved,
even with an extensively engineered bispecific antibody. However,
more routine and predictable immunogenicity risk assessment and
mitigation for engineered antibodies and other protein therapeutics
is still an urgent and much sought-after goal for drug developers12.
