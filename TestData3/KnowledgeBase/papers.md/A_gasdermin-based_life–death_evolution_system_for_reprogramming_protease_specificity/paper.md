### Front matter (pp. 1-3)

nature chemical biology A gasdermin-based life–death evolution system for reprogramming protease specificity Ziqi Gao 1,4, Tianzhen Li1,4, Hao Ye1,4, William Shu Ching Ngai2, Huijie Wang1, Peng R. Chen 2,3 & Jie Wang 1 Reprogramming the specificity of proteases toward alternative target sequences could enable an array of exciting applications, ranging from proteome editing to therapeutic interventions. Here we report an in vivo life–death selection system for protease reprogramming using the toxic N-terminal domain of gasdermin D (GD-N) protein as a selection marker. The approach is a modular system that can be used to cover the protease mutational diversity in the billions through only a few cycles of directed evolution. By inserting the desired cleavage sequence into the loop region of the GD-N protein, which is toxic to host bacteria cells, the system selects for efficient substrate cleavage—rendering GD-N nontoxic—by enrichment of bacteria in liquid culture. Using the tobacco etch virus protease (TEVp)

and corresponding substrate sequence as a model, we demonstrated that our platform could select and enrich an efficient protease variant millionfold after a single round of selection. We also evolve TEVp to cut sequences on target proteins with known pathological roles. The precise cleavage of a specific sequence by proteases is fundamental for regulating the functions, localizations and interactions of proteins and for creating new bioactive molecules by cutting zymogens or precursor proteins1,2. Protease activity is crucial for numerous physiological and pathological processes, such as programmed cell death3, the blood coagulation cascade4 and pathogen infection5. Programmable and sequence-specific cleavage enzymes such as Cas9 are cornerstones for gene editing, making possible almost unlimited possibilities for cutting, changing or reorganizing the genome; therefore, reprogramming the specificity of proteases to cut a new target protein could enable an array of exciting applications, ranging from proteome editing to therapeutic interventions6–9. For example, reprogrammed proteases could allow knocking down or out a target protein, on-demand activation of a desired signal transduction cascade and the elimination of pathological proteins9–13.

Restriction endonucleases and ligases have long been used for the cleavage and ligation of DNA. However, they are not considered gene-editing tools because these enzymes recognize specific DNA sequences that are only 6–8 bases long14 and such specificity is insufficient for selective targeting across the billions of base pairs in an entire human genome (Fig. 1a). In contrast, the human proteome consists of approximately 20,000 different protein-encoding genes and the length of all protein sequences in the proteome combined is roughly 10 million, indicating that target sequences of 5–7 aa are usually sufficient to achieve specificity in the proteome landscape15. Therefore, establishing a robust and straightforward selection method that can accomplish a saturated exploration of the mutational space in the substrate-binding pocket would be a major breakthrough for the development of protein-editing enzymes. One appealing approach is using a life–death selection system that directly links host growth to protease activity without any complex genetic pathway construction or multiple-step coupling; such a platform could effectively cover an extensive mutational space within a few rounds of selection with a minimalistic design to facilitate its adjustability, modularity and functionality.

To realize this vision, we report here an in vivo life–death selection system to reprogram the sequence recognition site of a protease. The selection system was optimized to be efficient, robust, stress tunable and capable of achieving millionfold enrichment of variants with the desired function in a single round of selection. We demonstrate that this approach can evolve the parent protease to reprogram its target sites within the entire proteome. Such an approach could effectively enable the editing of proteins, analogous to how nucleases are used to edit DNA sequences within the genome (Fig. 1a). To specifically target and cut a unique sequence on a protein of interest, it is essential to precisely alter the recognition sequence of a protease. Therefore, reprogramming a protease to cut a specific sequence requires a highly efficient and robust evolution system capable of handling a large amount of mutational diversity8,9,16,17. To evolve protease specificity in the proteome, exploring a billion-level mutational space is usually necessary, which is challenging for the traditional fluorescent screening or yeast display approaches11,16,18. This resulted in the evolution of proteases that have long been limited to enhancing activity and making minor alterations to the substrate sequences. Until recently, the phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) platform, which coupled protease activity to the lifecycle of bacteriophages, facilitated protease reprogramming that could achieve substantial changes in their substrate recognition sequences8,9. ### Results (pp. 3-9)

Development of selection system with tunable lethality based on N-terminal domain of gasdermin D (GD-N) We aim to construct a two-component selection system in Escherichia coli to couple protease activity with host growth. In brief, we inserted the target cutting sequence into the loop region of GD-N, a toxic protein that induces growth stress (Fig. 1b)20–22. Protease variants that recognize the target sequence destroy the toxic GD-N and the host bacteria harboring such variants become enriched in the liquid culture (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1a). The first objective was to identify a toxic protein that can provide tunable stress for E. coli growth. Inspired by the programable cell death processes in mammalian cells, which is triggered by a cascade of caspase-mediated cleavage events, we prioritized building an artificial ‘programable cell death’ system in E. coli by using the cell death trigger protein. We then focused on GD, a cell death trigger in pyroptosis. The full-length GD (GD-F) is activated by proteolytic cleavage releasing GD-N, which forms oligomers that create pores that serve as channels facilitating substance exchange across the cell membrane; this process is the key contributor to executing pyroptosis in mammalian cells21,23–25.

As this mechanism was well established in mammalian cells, we next tried to demonstrate whether the expression of GD-N in E. coli could induce cell death or cell growth stress (Fig. 1c). To this end, we expressed the GD-N protein in E. coli and found that the growth was strongly suppressed (Fig. 1c and Extended Data Fig. 1b,c), as evidenced by the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of the bacteria expressing GD-N remaining below 0.1 after 12 h of culturing. By equipping this highly toxic GD-N protein, the evolution system could be used for the selection of a highly active protease from a low-activity parent variant. However, in the sequence specificity reprogramming of a protease, the initial activity of a protease mutant would be very low. Therefore, this highly toxic GD-N protein was not suitable for the initial evolution of a protease to recognize an alternative sequence. To establish a stress-tunable evolution system that could be applied for the selection of the weakly active protease against the alternative target sequences, we constructed a toxicity-attenuated GD-N protein by fusing a SUMO tag to the N terminus (SUMO–GD-N). The toxicity of SUMO–GD-N is moderate, which is suitable as the selection marker (Fig. 2b). We also added four substitutions (R137A, K145A, R151A and R153A) to SUMO–GD-N to construct a nontoxic variant or dead mutant20,26 of GD-N (SUMO–GD-N-4A) as the negative control (Fig. 1d,e and Extended Data Fig. 1d).

We next checked the mechanism of the toxicity form the GD-N protein. As expected, using a GFP-tagged construct (GD-N–GFP), we found that GD-N–GFP accumulated on the E. coli membrane (Extended Data Fig. 1e). We also observed that the expression of GD-N (Fig. 1f) and SUMO–GD-N (Fig. 1g and Extended Data Fig. 1f) triggered different degrees of membrane structural damage, consistent with their toxicity to bacterial growth21,23. Additionally, we further determined that the selection stress from SUMO–GD-N could be tuned by altering its expression level and by changing the antibiotic concentration in the culture medium (Fig. 1h). This continuously tunable performance provides a wide selection window for the evolution of proteases with different activity levels, indicating that the SUMO–GD-N protein is a suitable inducer of selective stress to construct a protease evolution platform. Validation of the GD-N-based protease reprogramming system Next, to couple protease cleavage activity to host bacterial growth, we searched for a cleavage site on the SUMO–GD-N protein in which to insert the target sequence for the evolved proteases that met the following requirements: (1) insertion of the sequence must not affect the toxicity of the GD-N protein; (2) the insertion sequence must be sufficiently exposed so that it can be efficiently cleaved by the protease; and (3) the cleaved GD-N protein fragment must lose its pore-forming ability to render it nontoxic to the host bacteria. Therefore, we analyzed the crystal structure of the GD-N protein and identified several potential positions for insertion of the target sequence21,22.

Next, for proof of concept, we selected tobacco etch virus protease (TEVp) as our model protease to establish an evolutionary system for protease reprogramming. TEVp is characterized by a well-defined active site and a highly specific cleavage sequence, ENLYFQS, which is absent in both the E. coli and the human proteomes. This specificity confers a considerable advantage in minimizing off-target cleavage, making TEVp an ideal candidate for developing sequence-specific proteases through directed evolution27–29. The ENLYFQS sequence was inserted into nine positions on the SUMO–GD-N protein either through direct insertion or through insertion–deletion to construct 15 variants (P1–P15) (Fig. 2a and Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). We then evaluated the toxicity of the P1–P15 variants using SUMO– GD-N (wild type, WT) and the dead mutant as a control. We found that the P2, P4, P5, P9 and P12–P15 variants all failed to cause sufficient stress for E. coli growth, whereas the P1, P3, P6–P8, P10 and P11 variants caused remarkable selection stress to the host and this stress could be rescued by the expression of TEVp-WT (Fig. 2b and Extended Data Fig. 2c). To identify the most efficient SUMO–GD-N variant for protease evolution, we separately cotransformed the strains expressing P1, P3, P6–P8, P10 and P11 strains with TEVp-WT and mixed them in equal proportions into a single liquid culture. After the cultures were passaged three times at a 1:1,000 dilution, the proportion of P10-expressing cells was enriched to 53.1% of the culture (Fig. 2c,d, and Extended Data Fig. 3a), prompting us to select the P10 variant for further application. Next, immunoblot analysis demonstrated that the P10 variant could be efficiently cleaved by TEVp-WT (Fig. 2e) and the cleavage of P10 effectively rescued the bacterial growth defect in both liquid and solid media (Fig. 2f,g). Together, these results confirm that host bacterial growth can be used as a readout of the specific protease activity of TEVp for target sequence cleavage.

Next, to explore the selection window of this evolutionary system, we compared the growth curves of E. coli coexpressing TEVp-WT and GD-N-P10 with those coexpressing the catalytically inactive TEVp mutant C151A (TEVp-C151A) and GD-N-P10 under various temperature conditions. We observed that the bacteria expressing TEVp-WT exhibited much faster growth rates than those expressing TEVp-C151A, providing a distinct selection window for evolving our proteases. Notably, this selection window demonstrated a pronounced temperature dependence; at lower temperatures, the growth curves of the E. coli were extended and, correspondingly, the selection window was also broadened (Fig. 3a–d). This characteristic allowed for fine-tuning of the evolutionary selection pressure through temperature adjustments, offering a convenient framework for the evolution of proteases with varying activities. Subsequently, we evaluated the evolutionary efficiency of this platform for a fixed target sequence (ENLYFQS) and variable protease mutants. We conducted a model selection by mixing E. coli bacteria bearing TEVp-WT and catalytically inactive TEVp-C151A at a proportion ratio of 1:1–1:106 and, after only one round of culture and selection, we sequenced the population and found that the bacteria bearing TEVp-WT was dominantly enriched (Fig. 3e–g and Extended Data Fig. 3b,c).

Even when we started cultures with a 1:105 proportion of TEVp-WT to TEVp-C151A, the final proportion of TEVp-WT reached 94:6, which represented millionfold enrichment during the selection process (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). To evaluate the evolutionary efficiency of this platform for a fixed protease variant (TEVp-WT) and different target sequences, we performed a model selection by mixing E. coli expressing GD-N-P10 (ENLYFQS) and GD-N-WT at a 1:1–1:106 ratio. Bacteria expressing GD-N-P10 were dominantly enriched 4.56 × 105-fold after only one round of culture and selection (Fig. 3h–j and Extended Data Fig. 3d). These results demonstrated that this life–death selection system could efficiently enrich the desired protease variant capable of cleaving the target sequence. However, the system cannot exclude those variants with both on-target and off-target cleavage capability. Therefore, to exclude evolved variants that are selected for on the basis of potent on-target activity but that have undesirable off-target activity as well, we designed a negative selection system by inserting any identified specific off-target sequences that do not want to be cut between the N-terminal domain and the C-terminal domain of GD-F21. If a TEVp variant has off-target activity that cleaves the GD-F insertion sequence, the released GD-N protein would trigger E. coli death, thereby preventing enrichment of this variant during the evolution processes. We, therefore, picked nine positions for the insertion of the WT-TEVp substrates (ENLY- FQS) as a model off-target sequence (N1–N9) and used these variants for further screening. After coexpression of each GD-F variant and TEVp-WT, we identified three GD-F variants—N2, N3 and N7—that exhibited the expected toxicity, indicating that these three positions could be used to construct an efficient negative selection system (Extended Data Fig. 4).

By using the negative selection system, an evolved protease variant that cut any defined off-target sequence will be avoided. In summary, compared to other evolution systems that need complex genetic circuits or sophisticated infrastructure, these results demonstrate that this method is an efficient, robust and stress-tunable platform capable of selecting the desired protease variant from millions to billions of possibilities after just a few rounds of selection in culture. Evolution of TEVp to recognize and cleave mitochondrial ALAS1 We next selected ALAS1 as the target protein to evolve TEVp. ALAS1 is a mitochondrial protein that acts as the rate-determining enzyme of heme biosynthesis (Fig. 5a, left)36 and knockdown of ALAS1 in the liver mediated by small interfering RNA has been clinically applied to treat acute intermittent porphyria37,38. In addition, ALAS1 was recently demonstrated as the key protein to limit microRNA accumulation and ALAS1 suppression in the liver generally enhances synthetic small interfering RNA silencing activity, indicating its potential as general adjuvant in RNA interference therapy39. We hypothesized that our evolutionary protease selection platform could be used to reengineer TEVp precisely to cut mitochondrial ALAS1. Before we started evolution, we expressed an ALAS1 variant with the original TEVp-WT substrate sequence ENLY- FQS replacing the native HNIYVQA sequence at positions 551–557 in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5a). This modified ALAS1 harboring ENLYFQS was then incubated with TEVp-WT and immunoblotting analysis revealed a 70-kDa band below the native ALAS1 band (Fig. 5a, right), confirming that the original sequence at the 551–557 position of ALAS1 was accessible and could be cleaved by TEVp-WT.

TEVp-WT could not cleave the final MBP–HNIYVQA–GFP protein but showed weak activity toward three intermediate sequences (Fig. 5b), prompting us to design an evolutionary route for the final target sequence (Fig. 5c). Next, to verify the ability of TEVALAS1-2.1 to cut full-length ALAS1, we expressed WT ALAS1 protein in HEK293T cells. After incubating the cell lysate with TEVALAS1-2.1 for 3 hours, the expected 70-kDa band indicative of cleavage was observed through immunoblotting analysis (Fig. 5f). To verify the capability of TEVALAS1-2.1 to cleave in live cells, we fused a cell-penetrating artificial mitochondria-targeting peptide (CAMP) to the N terminus of TEVALAS1-2.1 (Fig. 5g) to deliver the protease into the mitochondria, where ALAS1 is located40,41. We expressed ALAS1 into HEK293T and confirmed cleavage of intracellular ALAS1 by immunoblotting analysis (Fig. 5h). Lastly, to demonstrate whether this protease will generate off-target cleavage on the proteome, we performed a comprehensive quantitative proteomic analysis of cell lysates treated with TEVALAS1-2.1 and the cell treated with TEVp dead mutant was used as the control. The quantitative proteomic analysis of cell lysates treated with TEVALAS1-2.1 revealed no notable proteome-wide changes compared to treatment with the TEV dead mutant, suggesting minimal off-target effects of the evolved TEVp (Fig. 5i). Collectively, these results demonstrate the potential of TEVALAS1-2.1 to cleave ALAS1 protein in liver, which might be further developed as an alternative strategy for treating acute intermittent porphyria treatment or could be further used as a general adjuvant in RNA interference therapy.

Altogether, these findings verify that TEVEZHIP-1.1 could cleave endogenous, intracellular EZHIP, again demonstrating the broad applicability of the selection platform to evolve highly specific proteases for potential clinical applications.

### Discussion (p. 9)

In this study, we developed an evolution platform to reprogram TEVp-WT specificity to expand the breadth of substrates that could be efficiently cleaved. We first evolved TEVp-WT to target the B cellspecific membrane receptor CD22, which is a critical ligand for immune response and B cell signaling transduction. The target sequence on CD22 harbored a 5-aa difference compared to the native TEVp-WT recognition sequence ENLYFQS, indicating that the recognition sequence of proteases can be markedly changed by this evolution system to recognize virtually any target sequence of interest within the proteome. We next demonstrated the evolution of TEVp to cleave the mitochondrial protein ALAS1, which is an essential protein for heme biosynthesis, and the nuclear protein EZHIP, which regulates histone methylation. These two cases confirmed protein targets within different intracellular membranes could also be cleaved by an evolved TEVp. Notably, we achieved targeted cleavage of these endogenous intracellular proteins in pathologically relevant cell lines, demonstrating the potential of this platform for in vivo proteome editing.

We demonstrated that the target sequences on these proteins could differ by up to 5 aa from the original TEVp-WT substrate sequence, indicating that our evolution system can broadly reprogram the specificity of proteases to enable precise manipulation of protein functions and interactions. Combining a substrate-walking strategy and our protease evolution platform could be extended to other proteases and targets, broadening the technology’s applications8,9. We also provide proof of concept that multiple protein delivery systems could facilitate targeted intracellular cleavage of endogenous proteins in live cells46,50. Analagous to the natural evolution process of antibodies, which are selected in vivo from diversities ranging between 1010 and 1012 to bind specific protein epitopes, we propose that directed evolution is a viable method for developing epitope-specific proteases for precise protein editing51. Just as B cells undergo extensive selection to produce highly specific antibodies, our evolution platform can achieve millionfold enrichment in a single selection round. With multiple rounds of library building and screening, our method can cover diversities exceeding 1 billion variants of proteases, theoretically enabling the evolution of proteases specific to virtually any target sequences. Considering the success of engineering antibodies for therapeutic applications, we envision that the field of evolved protease recognition holds vast potential for growth, with promising avenues for developing therapeutic, diagnostic tools and beyond.

Spot titer experiments Picked a single colony of E. coli into 3 ml of LB medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The culture was placed on ice and E. coli cells were collected by centrifugation (4 °C, 3,000 rpm, 10 min). The pellets were suspended in PBS whilst adjusting OD600 to approximately 1.0. Serial tenfold dilutions were made and, for every sample, 2 μl of each dilution was spotted on LB agar plates supplemented with antibiotics and inducers. The spots were allowed to dry.

Plates were incubated in 37 °C for 12–16 h. Membrane staining and imaging The BL21(DE3) containing recombinant plasmid pET28a-GD was induced by 2% arabinose and 0.5 mM IPTG at 30 °C for 16 h. Identification of suitable SUMO–GD-N variants for positive selection We generated a modified pET11(a) vector that deleted the LacI promoter and LacI regions. The mutagenesis plasmid encoding 6×His-tagged TEVp (containing S219V or S219E substitution) was assembled using the Gibson assembly protocol, named pTEV-S219V or pTEV-S219E.

The expression vectors pET28(b) encoding SUMO–GD-N and a dead mutant variant with four substitutions (R137A, K145A, R151A and R153A) were constructed. The positions (P1–P15) inserted or replaced with the TEVp-WT recognition sequence ENLYFQS according to the structure of the GD-N protein (Protein Data Bank (PDB) 6VFE) and 15 selection plasmids (P1–P15) were generated. These selection plasmids were transformed into BL21(DE3) chemically competent cell with or without pTEV-S219V by heat shock. A single colony of each type of strain was picked into 3 ml of LB medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm. The culture was inoculated into fresh LB medium contained antibiotic in a 1:1,000 ratio and cultivated at 37 °C with shaking. After 2 h, TEVp and SUMO–GD-N expression were induced with 0.5 mM IPTG. The group without IPTG was as control. The OD600 values of each culture were determined every 2 h.

Negative selection The expression vector pET28(b) encoding SUMO–GD was constructed. In this vector, positions N1–N9 were either inserted with or replaced by the TEVp-WT recognition sequence ENLYFQS on the basis of the structure of the GD protein (AF-P57764-F1). A total of nine negative selection plasmids (N1–N9) were generated. These plasmids were transformed into BL21(DE3) chemically competent cells with or without the pTEV-S219V plasmid by heat shock. Single colonies of each strain were picked and cultured overnight at 37 °C in 3 ml of LB medium with shaking at 220 rpm. The overnight culture was diluted 1:1,000 into fresh LB medium containing the appropriate antibiotic and grown at 37 °C with shaking. Proteomics analysis To evaluate potential off-target proteolysis by TEVALAS1-2.1 or TEVEZHIP-1 variants, proteomics analysis were performed according to previous methods with modifications53–55. After cell lysate and protein extraction, the proteomics study was performed to evaluate whether the TEVALAS1-2.1 or TEVEZHIP-1 variants resulted in notable off-target cleavage in the proteome. The HEK293T cell lysate was treated with TEVp-WT, TEVALAS1-2.1 or TEVEZHIP-1.1 for 1 h and then subjected to trypsinization, tandem mass tag (TMT) labeling and quantitative proteomic analysis. The sample treated with TEVp dead mutant was used as the control. The sample was prepared using SISPROT. The workflow of TMT-based quantitative proteomics analysis consisted of seven key steps. Firstly, 50 μl of protein sample was mixed with DTT and vortexed. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and allowed to cool to room temperature. IAA was then added and the reaction was carried out in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Additional DTT was added and the reaction continued at room temperature for 15 min. Secondly, two hydrophilic carboxylated magnetic beads, mixed in a 1:1 ratio, were incubated at 1,000 rpm and 24 °C for 10 min.

The protein was precipitated and the magnetic bead fraction was separated using magnetic isolation. Thirdly, the magnetic beads were resuspended in EPPS buffer, LysC and Trypsin were added and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with rotation for 16 h. Fourthly, after enzymatic digestion, the samples were transferred to a new tube and resuspended in EPPS buffer (pH 8.5). Fifthly, TMT18plex labeling was performed as indicated in the table below, with the corresponding samples. The reaction was carried out at room temperature for 1 h. Sixthly, following the reaction, 5% hydroxylamine solution was added and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min to terminate the reaction. The labeled samples were combined in equal amounts and desalted at 45 °C. Lastly, after drying, the samples were subjected to HPLC fractionation, resulting in 48 fractions, which were then combined into 24 fractions. The resulting peptide samples were dried and analyzed.

For liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/ MS) analysis, the peptide samples were resuspended in a solution of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water. LC was performed using a low-pH mobile phase separation on an Easy-nLC 1200 system coupled to a Q-Exactive HF-X Orbitrap MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For LC, mobile phase A was 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The column used was a reverse-phase analytical column (25 cm, inner diameter: 75 μm). The gradient settings were as follows: 0 min, 3% B; 5 s, 5% B; 24 min, 15% B; 45 min, 28% B; 52 min 30 s, 38% B; 52 min 35 s, 100% B; 65 min, 100% B; stop. The flow rate was maintained at 300 nl min−1. MS was performed in positive ion mode using the Orbitrap mass analyzer. Full-scan MS spectra were acquired in the range of 350–1,500 Da with a resolution of 60,000 (profile mode). Data-dependent acquisition was used for MS/ MS, with centroid mode for the spectra. The 15 most intense ion peaks were selected for secondary high-energy collision-induced dissociation fragmentation. MS/MS spectra were acquired at a resolution of 45,000.

Additional parameters included an isolation window of 0.7 m/z, default charge state of 2+, normalized collision energy of 35%, maximum injection time of 50 ms and dynamic exclusion set to 30.0 s. For data analysis, the raw data were processed using Proteome Discoverer (version 2.5, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The analysis was performed against the human UniProt FASTA database with an integration tolerance of 20 ppm. Carbamidomethylation was specified as a fixed modification for fragment ions. A false discovery rate of 1% was applied at the peptide spectrum match and peptide levels. Statistical significance was determined using a t-test.
